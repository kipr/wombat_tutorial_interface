## Overview

This will look familiar. A central gear, racks on either side, two arms closing on a 4-inch cube --- that is the grabber you built in Challenge 11.

The difference is in the title. Challenge 11 was about keeping the jaws parallel. This one is about *length*: getting the arms out past the edge of the machine, so the object does not have to be sitting inside the base to be grabbed.

{{< callout title="Core Insight" >}}
Racks do not only move things. A rack that slides out of its housing makes the machine longer than it was --- and if that housing is itself sliding, the two add up.
{{< /callout >}}

### By the end of this challenge you will be able to:

- Explain how sliding stages add their travel together.
- Measure the reach of a mechanism past its own [[FOOTPRINT|footprint]].
- Predict when an extending machine will tip, using center of gravity.
- Design a base that stays put while the arms are fully out and loaded.

## Phase 2 --- Concept: Stages Add Up

A rack sliding out of a housing has a limit: it can only travel as far as its own length before it falls out. One rack, one [[STAGE|stage]], one amount of reach.

Now put a second rack on the first, sliding along it. When both are extended, the second one starts from wherever the first one ended --- so the two travels add together.

{{< figrow tall=true >}}
- src: "mech20_linear_teeth-diagram-01.svg"
  alt: "Two extension systems compared. A single stage slides one bar out of a base and reaches a certain distance. Two stages, one carried on the other, reach twice as far from a base of the same size."
  caption: "Each stage starts where the one before it finished, so their travels add."
{{< /figrow >}}

### The Cost of Reaching Out

The moment your arms cross the edge of the base, the machine has an [[OVERHANG|overhang]] --- weight hanging out past the footprint, with nothing under it.

You met this rule in Challenge 2. If the [[CENTER OF GRAVITY|center of gravity]] stays over the base, the machine stands. Push it past the edge and the machine tips. Extending arms move weight outward, and adding an object at the far end moves it further still.

{{< callout title="The Failure You Should Expect" >}}
Your mechanism will probably work perfectly on the bench and then tip forward the first time you extend it fully with the cube in the jaws.

You have three tools for this, all from earlier challenges: widen the base, keep the heavy parts back over the base, or counterweight the far side. Challenge 5 was where you first used the third one.
{{< /callout >}}

Predict how far you will be able to reach before your machine tips. What will you do about it?

## Phase 3 --- Build the Bare Mechanism

Build the central gear and the racks first, with no arms attached. Get everything sliding cleanly before you add anything to it.

{{< callout title="Before You Go On" variant="navy" >}}
Run the mechanism through its full travel by hand five times. Both racks should move by the same amount and neither should skip a tooth at the end of its travel.

The specific problem with extending mechanisms is that a rack hanging far out of its housing is only supported at one end. It sags, lifts off the gear, and skips. If yours does that, it needs support further along, not more force.
{{< /callout >}}

Measure the footprint of your base, and the furthest the arms reach when fully extended. Record both.

## Phase 4 --- Engineering to Meet a Need

{{< callout title="The Need" >}}
Using your newly created LEGO contraption, modify it so that there is a motor attached to the central sprocket. Each lengthening piece should have an arm at 90° that would allow it to get wider or narrower to precisely grab a 4″ × 4″ × 4″ foam object.
{{< /callout >}}

### Mental Picture

Think of a telescoping ladder. Folded up it fits in the back of a van; extended it reaches the roof. Nothing about it grew --- the sections simply slid out of each other, and each one started where the last one ended.

### Constraints

Every one of these must be true for your design to count as finished.

1.  The base structure of the device must be stationary.
2.  The arms must be no less than 1½″ in height and 4″ in length.
3.  The arms must start in a position more narrow than the object to be grabbed.
4.  The foam cube must sit entirely outside the base's footprint. The arms have to reach past the edge of the machine to get to it.
5.  The base must not tip, slide, or lift while the arms are fully extended and holding the cube.
6.  The device must grab the cube on more than 90% of attempts, counted over at least 10 tries.
7.  Kit parts only.

## Phase 5 --- Test & Iterate

{{< callout title="Find the Tipping Point on Purpose" >}}
Do not wait for it to happen by accident. Extend the arms a little at a time with the cube held, and note the reach at which the base first starts to lift at the back.

That number is the real limit of your machine. Everything you do to fix it should be measured against moving that number further out.
{{< /callout >}}

## Phase 6 --- Connect: Where This Helps in Botball

{{< callout title="Transfer" >}}
A robot that has to drive to everything spends the match driving. A robot that can reach does not have to move as often.
{{< /callout >}}

Reaching also gets you to places a robot cannot go. If a scoring zone is behind a barrier or in a spot too tight to drive into, an arm that extends is the only way in.

Name a Botball task where reaching would beat driving over. What does staying put save you?

## Extension Challenges

### Extension A --- Add a Stage

- Add another extending stage and measure the new reach.
- Did the reach go up by the full amount you expected, or less? Where did the difference go?
- What happened to how steady the arms are at full extension?

{{< answer key="ext_a" label="Extension A" >}}

### Extension B --- Push the Tipping Point

- Record the reach at which your machine tips. Then try each fix in turn: widen the base, move heavy parts back, add a counterweight.
- Measure the new tipping reach after each change, one at a time.
- Which single change bought you the most, and was it the one you expected?

{{< answer key="ext_b" label="Extension B" >}}

### Extension C --- Heavier at Arm's Length

- Replace the foam cube with something noticeably heavier of the same size.
- Does your machine still hold it at full extension?
- Calculate the torque at the base pivot as you did in Challenge 18. Does the number explain what happened?

{{< answer key="ext_c" label="Extension C" >}}

### Extension D --- Retract Under Load

- Grab the cube at full extension and then pull the whole thing back in, still holding it.
- Is retracting harder or easier than extending? Why?
- Time both directions over five cycles and compare.
